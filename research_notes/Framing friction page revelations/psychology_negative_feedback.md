# Psychology of Delivering Negative, Self-Relevant (Personality-Flaw) Feedback

Scope note for the report writer: almost all of the evidence below comes from lab experiments with students, from clinical or health-message research, or from workplace and education settings. None of it tests an entertainment or astrology-style reading app directly. Where a claim rests on a secondary summary rather than the primary paper, that is flagged. Items marked "[background, unverified this session]" are well-known claims I could not source during this research. They are listed only in Inferences or Gaps and should not be cited as facts.

## 1. Barnum/Forer effect: why vague, double-sided statements feel accurate, and what negative content does to perceived accuracy

### Takeaway
People rate generic personality descriptions as highly accurate, especially when the description is mostly favourable, appears personalised, and comes from a trusted, authoritative source. Negative statements are accepted when they come in small doses inside a mostly positive profile, are hedged with phrases like "at times", or come from a high-status source. The evidence points to favourability raising acceptance. I found no clean primary estimate of how much each added negative item lowers it.

### Cited Findings
- Forer (1949): 39 psychology students rated an identical vignette, taken from a newsstand astrology book, as describing them, giving it an average of 4.30 on a 0–5 scale. The vignette itself contained negative items (e.g., "Your sexual adjustment has presented problems for you") — [Wikipedia summary of Forer 1949](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)
- Dickson & Kelly's (1985) review of the Barnum literature concludes that "the level of acceptance of Barnum profiles depends on the relevance and favorability of the profile and to some extent on the type of assessment utilized." Variables reviewed include generality and supposed relevance, favourability, assessment procedure, and the origin and format of the interpretation — [Dickson & Kelly 1985 via search abstract](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf)
- Statements about socially desirable traits are accepted as more accurate. Analyses that list mainly positive traits get higher accuracy ratings. The ratio of positive to negative trait assessments matters (Claridge et al., 2008, *Personality and Individual Differences*) — [Wikipedia, citing Dickson & Kelly 1985 and Claridge et al. 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)
- Furnham (2014) links the effect to the "Pollyanna principle": people "use or accept positive words of feedback more frequently than negative words of feedback" — [Wikipedia, citing Furnham 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)
- Negative assessments are more likely to be accepted when the presenter is seen as a high-status professional. People who are authoritarian, neurotic, or high in need for approval show the effect more — [Wikipedia, citing Furnham 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)
- Trust in the honesty of the feedback provider is a key factor in acceptance (Claridge et al., 2008). Belief in the evaluator's authority raises ratings (Dickson & Kelly, 1985) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)
- Ratings are higher when people believe the analysis was personalised. The most effective statements contain "at times" (e.g., "At times you feel very sure of yourself, while at other times you are not as confident"), because readers "read their own meaning into the statements" — [Wikipedia, citing Krauss-Whitbourne 2010 and Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)
- A study devoted specifically to negative content exists: Dmitruk, Collins & Clinger (1973), "The 'Barnum effect' and acceptance of negative personal evaluation," *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, 41, 192–194. I could not retrieve its results — [citation listing](https://ras.jes.su/psy/s020595920014245-5-1-en)
- Halperin et al. (1976) studied how source status and message favourability affect acceptance of personality feedback. A later study found that links between personality traits and the Barnum effect are moderated by the type of feedback given — [search summary of Vilnius University paper](https://www.zurnalai.vu.lt/psichologija/article/download/4893/3148/)
- Furnham & Schofield (1987), "Accepting personality test feedback: A review of the Barnum effect" (*Current Psychological Research & Reviews*, 6(2), 162–178), has been called the most comprehensive review of the topic. The authors questioned whether false-feedback studies are relevant to real clinical feedback — [search summary](https://www.zurnalai.vu.lt/psichologija/article/download/4893/3148/); [Springer DOI](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF02686623)
- The Barnum effect is one case of the broader "acceptance phenomenon," the tendency to "accept almost any bogus personality feedback" (Tobacyk et al., 2010, *Journal of Personality Assessment*) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnum_effect)

### Inferences
- Favourability is a main driver of perceived accuracy, so a reading that leads with a reader's downsides will likely be rated as less accurate than a mostly flattering one, even if it is equally true. A mostly positive frame with a minority of negative content follows the classic high-acceptance pattern, and Forer's own vignette was built that way.
- Double-sided "at times X, at other times Y" phrasing works because every reader can find themselves in it. For a downsides page this cuts both ways. It raises felt accuracy and lowers felt threat, but it also lowers informational value: the reader learns nothing that separates them from anyone else.
- Source credibility and authority raise acceptance of negative statements. An app that presents its reading as coming from a coherent system with confident, specific language may get negative content accepted more easily. This is an ethical tension, because the same mechanism makes bogus feedback convincing.
- Barnum acceptance is high for false feedback too. High felt accuracy therefore does not show that a reading is valid, and the app should not treat user agreement as evidence that its downsides are true.

### Gaps
- I found no primary effect size for "adding N negative items lowers accuracy by X." The Dmitruk et al. (1973) and Furnham & Schofield (1987) full texts could not be retrieved (both PDFs were scanned or compressed and unreadable).
- Snyder, Shenkel & Lowery's (1977) review and Glick, Gottesman & Jolton (1989), on astrology believers versus sceptics accepting horoscopes, could not be found in this session. [Background, unverified this session: Glick et al. reportedly found that a favourable horoscope increased astrology belief even among sceptics.]

## 2. Self-verification vs self-enhancement: do people want accurate negative feedback?

### Takeaway
Under the right conditions, people prefer feedback that confirms their self-view, even a negative one, and they rate a confirming evaluator as "knowing them better." The pull is ambivalent: people still like favourable feedback emotionally. The preference for self-confirming feedback appears once people have time to access their self-views. For a flaw the reader already recognises in themselves, naming it can raise felt accuracy and trust rather than lower it.

### Cited Findings
- Swann, Stein-Seroussi & Giesler (1992): when choosing between evaluators, people with positive self-views preferred favourable partners and people with negative self-views preferred unfavourable partners. Reviews describe more than a dozen replications across labs and methods (secondary citation within Swann lab papers) — [Swann lab review](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/swann/files/2016/03/sw_int_studies05.pdf); [Simply Psychology summary](https://www.simplypsychology.org/self-verification-theory.html)
- The choice is often ambivalent. A low-self-esteem participant said they liked the favourable evaluation but chose the unfavourable evaluator because that evaluator seemed to know them better. A quick pull toward positive feedback comes first, and the preference for self-confirming feedback "emerges later when people access their self-views" — [Swann, "trouble" paper](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/swann/files/2016/03/swann97_trouble.pdf)
- Giesler, Josephs & Swann (1996): 82% of depressed participants chose unfavourable feedback, versus 64% of low-self-esteem participants — [Giesler, Josephs & Swann](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/swann/files/2016/03/gies_jos_sw.pdf)
- Children (Reijntjes et al., 2010): pre-adolescents with negative self-views preferred negative feedback, but after a prior success experience they were as likely as positive-self-view peers to prefer positive feedback — [Self-verification strivings in children](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/telch/files/2015/02/Self-Veriﬁcation-Strivings-in-Children.pdf)
- The mechanism is debated: a desire for coherence and control, or a belief that self-consistent feedback is more accurate (2021 *Psychological Science* article) — [Psychological Science 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976211049439)
- Wood, Perunovic & Lee (2009, *Psychological Science*, 20, 860–866): participants with low self-esteem who repeated "I'm a lovable person" felt worse than controls, and felt better when allowed to focus on how the statement was both true and not true. High-self-esteem participants benefited only slightly. Positive self-statements "backfire for the very people who 'need' them the most" — [abstract via Contextual Science](https://contextualscience.org/publications/wood_perunovic_lee_2009); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/07/090702110503.htm)

### Inferences
- Pure flattery carries a credibility cost. Readers who know they have a flaw (e.g., impatience) may find a reading that omits it less trustworthy. Naming recognisable downsides can increase the sense that the reading "gets me," which fits the Swann "knows me better" finding.
- Wood et al. (2009) suggests over-positive statements may harm readers with low self-regard, who reject them, while "both true and not true" framing was better tolerated. This supports balanced, two-sided descriptions over uniformly glowing ones.
- Readers bring different self-views to the same reading. People who see themselves negatively may latch onto the negative content and use it to confirm their self-view, which bears on the rumination risk in section 8. Pairing a flaw with a recent strength or success (analogous to Reijntjes's prior-success manipulation) may soften that.

### Gaps
- No study found tests self-verification with horoscope or typology content, where the "self-view" is partly built by the app itself.

## 3. Self-affirmation: does affirming strengths or values first reduce defensiveness?

### Takeaway
Yes, with small but reliable effects. When self-integrity is affirmed, people process threatening self-relevant information less defensively. The best-supported manipulation is writing about one's own cherished values, not receiving praise from someone else.

### Cited Findings
- Cohen & Sherman (2014), "The psychology of change: Self-affirmation and social psychological intervention," *Annual Review of Psychology*, 65, 333–371. A companion lecture describes brief, low-cost values-affirmation writing exercises that reduce stress and improve performance in some groups — [UCSB Sherman lab listing](https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/sherman/david/node/323); [Annual Reviews lecture](https://annualreviews.org/content/multimedia/10.1146/do.multimedia.2013.12.17.238)
- Sherman & Cohen (2002, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*): resistance to threatening information comes largely from a motive to protect self-worth. Self-affirmed people were more willing to accept threatening information and to change beliefs or behaviour. Defensive biases protect self-worth but can hinder change and increase conflict — [search summary of Sherman & Cohen 2002](https://ed.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/self_defense.pdf)
- Epton, Harris, Kane, van Koningsbruggen & Sheeran (2015) meta-analysis of 144 experimental tests (health messages): self-affirmation increased message acceptance (d+ = .17, CI .03–.31), intentions (d+ = .14) and behaviour (d+ = .32). The effects are small but comparable to other health-behaviour interventions — [EHPS summary](https://www.ehps.net/ehp/index.php/contents/article/view/271); [VU research portal](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/the-impact-of-self-affirmation-on-health-behavior-change-a-meta-a/)
- Moderators, from the author's blog: self-affirmation was more effective when the threat was proximal — [SPSP blog by Epton](https://spsp.org/news-center/character-context-blog/self-affirmation-changes-health-behavior)
- A separate, smaller meta-analysis of 16 studies found small effects on intentions (d+ = .26) and behaviour (d+ = .27), and effects on intentions did not predict effects on behaviour — [search summary](https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/self-affirmation-and-responses-to-health-messages-a-meta-analysis/)

### Inferences
- The analogue for an app is to first remind the reader of a valued strength or core value, before presenting the downside. Evidence comes from health and education messaging, and the manipulation there is usually self-generated (the reader writes about their own value). Opening a reading with app-written praise is a weaker analogue and may behave more like the "sandwich" (section 4).
- An interactive step, such as "Pick the value that matters most to you" or "Which of these strengths do you recognise?", would be closer to the validated paradigm than passive praise.
- Effect sizes are small (d about 0.15–0.3). Affirmation reduces defensiveness, but it does not make harsh content safe on its own.

### Gaps
- No direct test of self-affirmation before personality or typology flaws in a consumer app.
- Cohen & Sherman's (2014) review discusses when affirmations backfire, but I could not retrieve the full text to report those boundary conditions.

## 4. Feedback framing: sandwich, strengths-as-overuse, person vs process, specific vs global, descriptive vs evaluative

### Takeaway
Feedback that draws attention to the self (identity, worth) rather than to the task or behaviour reliably does worse. More than a third of feedback interventions in Kluger & DeNisi's meta-analysis made performance worse, and person-focused criticism produces helplessness in children. The "feedback sandwich" mainly changes how feedback feels, not what people do with it. Framing a flaw as an overused strength ("two sides of the same trait") comes from practitioner leadership research and is plausible, but it has not been rigorously tested and is contested.

### Cited Findings
- Kluger & DeNisi (1996, *Psychological Bulletin*), meta-analysis of 607 effect sizes and 23,663 observations: the mean effect of feedback interventions is d = .41, but more than one third of interventions decreased performance. This could not be explained by sampling error, feedback sign, or existing theories — [Kluger & DeNisi abstract](https://pluto.huji.ac.il/%7Emskluger/files/Download/Kluger%20&%20%20DeNisi%20abstract.doc); [HUJI listing](https://scholars.huji.ac.il/node/1802)
- Feedback Intervention Theory: feedback effectiveness declines as attention moves up the hierarchy from the task toward meta-task, self-related processes. Positive feedback could have the same kind of harmful effect as negative feedback — [Kluger & DeNisi PDF](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Marking%20and%20Feedback/The%20effects%20of%20feedback%20interventions.pdf); [Psych Safety field guide](https://explore.psychsafety.com/n/kluger-denisi-1996/)
- Kamins & Dweck (1999, *Developmental Psychology*): 5–6-year-olds (Study 1, n = 67) received person, outcome, or process criticism after role-played setbacks. Person criticism (and person praise in Study 2, n = 64) produced significantly more helpless responses, including self-blame, lower self-ratings, negative mood and less persistence, on all dependent measures than process feedback. Outcome feedback fell in between. The authors concluded that person feedback fosters contingent self-worth — [Kamins & Dweck PDF](https://people.uncw.edu/hungerforda/Infancy/PDF/Person%20versus%20process%20praise%20and%20criticism.pdf); [Scinapse abstract](https://scinapse.io/papers/2154797511)
- Caveat: Cimpian, Arce, Markman & Dweck (2007) found that even subtle wording such as "You are a good drawer" (generic, person) versus "You did a good job drawing" (process) changed children's responses. The review that cites it describes the effects as similar, so the exact direction should be checked in the original — [Keele repository review](https://keele-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/453043)
- Feedback sandwich: Parkes, Abercrombie & McCarty (*Advances in Health Sciences Education*, 2012/2013; the year differs across sources) found that people who got sandwich feedback believed it would help them more, but performed no better than those who got plain criticism, and no worse — [Wikipedia: Compliment sandwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compliment_sandwich); [UTAS blog](https://blogs.utas.edu.au/snm-pep/?p=1134)
- Procházka, Ovcari & Ďurinik (2020, *Learning and Motivation*), n = 91 students: sandwich feedback on maths problems led to more practice and more problems solved. The authors call this partial evidence, because it cannot separate the effect of sequence from the mere presence of positive statements — [Macquarie listing](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/sandwich-feedback-the-empirical-evidence-of-its-effectiveness/)
- A 2022 IAFOR study found no effect of the sandwich on self-reported motivation — [IAFOR paper](https://papers.iafor.org/?p=65484)
- Kaplan & Kaiser (HBR 2009; book *Fear Your Strengths*, 2013) argue that most leaders are "lopsided": they overuse favoured qualities (forceful vs enabling, strategic vs operational), and strengths taken too far become liabilities. Their Leadership Versatility Index is a 360-degree instrument. They give three reasons for overuse: a more-is-better mentality, a skewed mental model, and a faulty gauge. Enron's Jeff Skilling is their example — [Integral Leadership Review](https://integralleadershipreview.com/9387-robert-e-kaplan-and-robert-b-kaiser-fear-your-strengths/); [Training mag](https://trainingmag.com/?p=11950)
- Contested: a Zenger Folkman white paper explicitly takes "an entirely opposite point of view" to the claim that strengths taken too far become weaknesses. A book review also faulted Kaplan & Kaiser for lacking a concrete action plan — [Zenger Folkman white paper](https://data.zengerfolkman.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/Can-Strengths-Be-Taken-Too-Far_WP-2019.pdf); [Portland Book Review](https://portlandbookreview.com/fear-your-strengths-what-you-are-best-at-could-be-your-biggest-problem/)

### Inferences
- The strongest evidence-based rule for a downsides page is to describe the behaviour or pattern and the situations that trigger it ("When plans change late, you tend to dig in"), not a verdict on the person's identity or worth ("You are stubborn"). This follows from Kluger & DeNisi's self-attention finding and Kamins & Dweck's person-vs-process finding. Both come from performance and education settings.
- Framing a flaw as the shadow side of a strength the reader already accepts ("the same drive that makes you X can tip into Y when overdone") does three useful things. It ties the negative to a valued self-aspect, which works like a built-in affirmation. It is more two-sided, and so more Barnum-acceptable. It implies a dial the reader can turn, not a fixed defect. The empirical support for the overuse model itself comes from leadership 360 data and is disputed, so present it as a framing device, not a validated science.
- A sandwich-like structure will probably make a reading feel kinder and more helpful, but evidence that it changes what readers do is weak. Since the goal is acceptance and felt accuracy, which are perceptions, the sandwich's main known effect happens to be the one wanted. If the sandwich is formulaic, readers may learn to discount the praise; this is a practitioner claim, not found in the studies above.
- Specific, concrete descriptions are generally recommended over global ones. Kluger & DeNisi's task-focus finding supports this indirectly, but in this session I did not find a primary study isolating specific vs global flaw descriptions (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- No primary study retrieved on specific vs global negative personality feedback or descriptive vs evaluative language. [Background, unverified this session: Watkins's work and attribution research (Abramson et al.'s reformulated learned-helplessness model) link global and stable attributions for negatives to depression risk.]
- Kluger & DeNisi's moderator details could only be confirmed from the abstract.

## 5. Fixed-trait labels and self-fulfilling prophecies

### Takeaway
Labels shape behaviour. Telling people they already have a trait changes behaviour more than urging them to change, so a negative trait label ("you are lazy") risks becoming self-confirming. Process and behaviour framing reduces this risk. Labels are not destiny, though: people can and do reject them.

### Cited Findings
- Miller, Brickman & Bolen (1975): children responded more to positive attribution ("you are tidy" or already have the virtue) than to persuasive appeals urging them to change (secondary reader's note, not the original paper) — [Goodreads note on *The Person and the Situation*](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/18844037-the-person-and-the-situation/15827252-an-te/2c712280-d5a4-4adb-9924-b84af5691c8c)
- Applying a label can produce behaviour consistent with that label. Self-fulfilling prophecies are not inevitable, and people can reject labels (textbook summary of Rosenthal & Jacobson-style work) — [UNE social psychology blog](https://blog.une.edu.au/socialpsychology/?p=541); [tutor2u](https://tutor2u.net/sociology/topics/self-fulfilling-prophecy)
- Person-focused criticism (identity labels) fosters contingent self-worth and helplessness in children — [Kamins & Dweck 1999](https://people.uncw.edu/hungerforda/Infancy/PDF/Person%20versus%20process%20praise%20and%20criticism.pdf)

### Inferences
- Attribution works in both directions. If positive labels pull behaviour toward the label, negative identity labels plausibly do too. An astrology-style reading that says "Scorpios are jealous" fixes the flaw by birth date, which is the most fixed and least controllable framing possible. That is the worst case under both the helplessness and the labelling findings.
- Safer framing: describe a tendency or pattern ("there's a pull toward...") that shows up in certain conditions, and pair it with a positive attribution the reader can grow into ("you have the capacity for..."). This uses the Miller et al. attribution effect constructively.

### Gaps
- No study found on whether astrology or typology labels (e.g., MBTI type, zodiac sign) produce self-fulfilling behaviour. [Background, unverified this session: there is literature suggesting astrology believers' self-descriptions drift toward their sign's traits (e.g., work by Eysenck & Nias and van Rooij), but it could not be sourced here.]
- The Miller et al. (1975) original was not retrieved.

## 6. Psychological reactance and preachiness: "you should" and autonomy-supportive language

### Takeaway
Directive, controlling language ("you must", "you should") raises perceived threat to freedom, anger and counter-arguing. Choice-enhancing language ("you could", "consider") reduces reactance. In some studies reactance shows up in attitudes and intentions more than in measured behaviour.

### Cited Findings
- Miller, Lane, Deatrick, Young & Potts (2007, *Human Communication Research*, 33, 219–240) tested controlling language, lexical concreteness and restoration of freedom in health promotion messages. "Controlling" language is also described in the literature as dogmatic or forceful: directive, explicit commands such as "you must" — [citation via LSE paper](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/108999/2/19485506211005582_1_.pdf); [catalogue DOI 10.1111/j.1468-2958.2007.00297.x](https://katalog.adlr.link/Record/ai-49-aHR0cDovL2R4LmRvaS5vcmcvMTAuMTExMS9qLjE0NjgtMjk1OC4yMDA3LjAwMjk3Lng?lng=en)
- COVID-19 message study (2021): commanding messages felt threatening and drew anger and negative evaluations, but affected only intentions, with no evidence of behavioural reactance — [LSE eprint](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/108999/2/19485506211005582_1_.pdf)
- Emergency-preparedness experiment: choice-restricting language increased perceived freedom threat and reactance. The authors recommend choice-enhancing language — [Disaster Medicine & Public Health Preparedness](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/disaster-medicine-and-public-health-preparedness/article/utility-of-choiceenhancing-language-in-emergency-preparedness-messages-an-application-of-psychological-reactance-theory/D074CE8FF67B652CAE8393EDF977160F)
- A 2026 analysis led by Long Ma (press coverage only): most reactance studies manipulate threat using directive features, and low-threat messages typically use words like "could" or "consider" — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/forceful-language-makes-people-resist-health-advice/); [Gigazine](https://www.gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260123-forceful-language-health-advice-reactance)

### Inferences
- In flaw sections, avoid imperatives and "you need to / you should / you must". Use invitational, choice-preserving phrasing ("one thing you might try...", "if you want to...", "some people with this pattern find..."). This matches autonomy-supportive practice in Self-Determination Theory and motivational interviewing, but see Gaps on sourcing.
- An unrequested reading that pairs a criticism with a command adds two threats: one to self-image and one to freedom. Keeping the description of the flaw separate from any optional suggestion should reduce combined defensiveness.

### Gaps
- Primary SDT and motivational interviewing sources (Deci & Ryan; Miller & Rollnick) were not retrieved this session. [Background, unverified this session: MI emphasises asking permission before giving information, an "elicit–provide–elicit" structure, and avoiding the "righting reflex". Brehm's 1966 reactance theory is the origin of the construct.]

## 7. Therapy and coaching practice: DBT validation and change, shadow work, radical candor, NVC

### Takeaway
Clinical traditions converge on a sequence: validate first (show the pattern makes sense given the person's makeup or history), then name the cost and invite change. Experimental support is thinnest for the specific techniques. The DBT rationale is that high emotional arousal blocks information processing, so validation should come before change talk.

### Cited Findings
- Linehan's biosocial model holds that high emotional arousal interferes with processing information, solving problems and focusing on the present. This is the rationale for validating before pushing change — [clinical slides on DBT](https://cls.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3019/2012/07/SLIDES-Self-Harm-11_5_2012.pdf)
- Linehan's six validation levels run from being present, through accurate reflection and naming unstated feelings, to validating in terms of past history and of present circumstances, up to radical genuineness. Use the highest level possible — [PsychCentral summary](https://psychcentral.com/blog/emotionally-sensitive/2012/02/understanding-the-levels-of-validation)
- Experimental (dissertation, n = 130, varying borderline features): invalidating, but not validating, feedback during a stressful task delayed recognition of emotional stimuli. Emotional sensitivity was context-dependent — [UNR dissertation](https://scholarwolf.unr.edu/entities/dissertation/725b2f37-8918-4133-9127-c7aa7efee8c6)
- As of the Rutgers study, "there have been no studies designed to look at therapist use or impact of specific [validation levels]" — [Rutgers record](https://rucore.libraries.rutgers.edu/rutgers-lib/51242/record/)

### Inferences
- The level 5 analogue ("this makes sense given...") maps neatly onto a reading. Explain why the flaw follows from the reader's temperament or chart before naming its cost, e.g., "Because you process deeply, quick decisions feel unsafe, so you can stall." This is validation plus a causal story, which also increases felt accuracy.
- The other traditions point the same way, but in this session I found no empirical evidence for them (see Gaps): Jungian shadow work treats the flaw as a disowned part to integrate, radical candor pairs caring personally with challenging directly, and nonviolent communication uses observation without evaluation.

### Gaps
- No sources were retrieved for Jungian shadow work, Kim Scott's Radical Candor, or Rosenberg's Nonviolent Communication. These are practitioner frameworks with little controlled evidence. [Background, unverified this session: NVC separates observation from evaluation; Radical Candor's 2x2 contrasts "ruinous empathy" and "obnoxious aggression".] Treat them as design heuristics, not evidence.

## 8. Wellbeing risks: rumination, distress, safe messaging

### Takeaway
Repeatedly dwelling on negative self-relevant content can be constructive or harmful. Harm is associated with negative valence, unhelpful context, and abstract, evaluative ("why am I like this?") rather than concrete, process-focused thinking. Vulnerable readers (low self-esteem, depressed) are the most likely to choose and believe negative feedback, and the most likely to be hurt by both overly positive and overly negative self-statements.

### Cited Findings
- Watkins (2008, *Psychological Bulletin*, 134, 163–206), "Constructive and unconstructive repetitive thought": unconstructive outcomes include depression, anxiety and health problems. Constructive outcomes include recovery from upsetting events, planning, recovery from depression and health behaviours. The proposed moderators are valence, context, and level of construal (abstract vs concrete) — [Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/11162); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/2672052/)
- 82% of depressed participants chose unfavourable self-feedback — [Giesler, Josephs & Swann 1996](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/swann/files/2016/03/gies_jos_sw.pdf)
- Positive self-statements made low-self-esteem participants feel worse — [Wood, Perunovic & Lee 2009](https://contextualscience.org/publications/wood_perunovic_lee_2009)
- Person-focused criticism produced self-blame and lower mood in children — [Kamins & Dweck 1999](https://people.uncw.edu/hungerforda/Infancy/PDF/Person%20versus%20process%20praise%20and%20criticism.pdf)

### Inferences
- Concrete, situational, behaviour-level descriptions with a "what helps" next step match Watkins's constructive mode. Abstract, global verdicts ("you are fundamentally X") match the unconstructive mode.
- The readers most likely to seek out and engage with downsides pages (negative self-view, depressed) are the ones Swann and Giesler show prefer confirming negative feedback. A page that is endlessly scrollable or "brutally honest" could feed a negative self-verification loop. Design mitigations: cap the number of flaws shown, always pair each one with a counterweight, avoid diagnostic or clinical terms, and avoid language about worth or fate.
- Readings should never imply that a flaw is unchangeable, dooms relationships, or is a reason for others to reject the reader. Global, stable, worth-related negatives are the most clearly hazardous category in this evidence base.
