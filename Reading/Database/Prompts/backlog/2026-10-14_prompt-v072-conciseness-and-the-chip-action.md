## The takeaway, as the owner put it

Batch 1 landed on 2026-10-14 and the owner read three rows against the station: every v0.7.1 passage won. The next complaint was the sentence itself: v0.7.1 sentences run as compounds of two or three clauses, and the owner asked for shorter sentences with fewer clauses, phrases where a clause only carries a logical relation, and no trailing "which". Iteration 8 of the blind samples re-cut the three rows that way and the owner took them. Then on the Alchemist's "Never quite ready" row the owner asked three things that became the other rules of this item: whether overpreparation is a cost to the reader rather than to others (the answer: all three angles stand, and the cost to the reader is the missed opportunity, so that passage must say so outright); what the point was of "reading" as the passage's action (there was none: reading was one instance of the chip's action carried over from the drive passage, and a reader who over-plans or over-rehearses never saw themselves); and, ruled on the spot, that the passage's main action is the action the chip states, with no delegate verb and no metaphor standing in for it.

## The owner's rulings, quoted

> Now we need to work on the conciseness of the sentences in v0.7.1. Simplify compound sentences into shorter sentences with less clauses, and use phrases as much as possible. Example: "You trust the version you suffered for more than the one that simply came, which puts a price on what was free" --> "which puts a price on what was free" is redundant.

(2026-10-14, on the three batch 1 examples; iteration 8 of `ComparativeAnalysis/Evaluations/handoff-geng-golden-2026-10-01/blind-rewrite-samples.md` is the re-cut the owner accepted: "Better.")

> Is "Never Quite Ready" a cost to one's self or cost to others? Overpreparation seems a cost against one's self on missing opportunities.

> Good, I like the 3 aspects all seen here. Now I want Cost to you to have Missing Opportunity more revealed and explicitly stated as the main point. Otherwise this sentence reads a bit implicit.

> What is the point of using Reading as the main action here?

> Remember to use what the phrases states as the main action instead of using metaphors. For example, Never gets ready needs the explanation to have "Getting Ready" as the main action but not any other verbs as delegates or metaphors which deviates from the main action that adds confusion to the user.

(2026-10-14, on the 土_偏印 row; the re-cuts the owner accepted, "the re-cuts read better", are the draft exemplars below.)

## The draft exemplars (the 土_偏印 row, the Alchemist, friction, "Never quite ready")

Each re-cut under rules 1 to 4; the action is getting ready in all three; the cost to the reader is the chance that closes, stated in the last sentence; the cost to others is the wait, at its real size. Before is the batch 1 v0.7.1 text (`Reading/Database/Rewrites/fable-v071/`); After is the draft.

### drive · 45 words

Before: One more source always feels wiser than a first try. You have more Earth than your core gets through, so you keep taking in rather than putting out, since intake feels like progress. Keep in mind that you will not read your way to ready, because starting is the only thing that gets you there.

After: One more pass always feels wiser than a first try. You have more Earth than your core gets through, so you get ready instead of getting started. Keep in mind that you will not prepare your way to ready, because only starting gets you there.

### cost_self · 41 words

Before: Reading up on a thing is where you feel most in control. Since the next page asks nothing of you while the first try asks everything, you stay in the pages past the point of use. Remember, you pay in real time, and your body keeps the count even when you lose it.

After: Getting ready is where you feel most in control. One more pass asks nothing of you, while the first try asks everything. Remember that every chance has a closing date, and most of yours close while you are still getting ready.

### cost_others · 44 words

Before: Whenever you owe someone an answer, you give them your preparation instead of the answer. Being ready counts for more with you than being on time, which puts the people who asked on your clock. Remember that you were asked for the rough thing you have, and you keep them waiting on polish.

After: Whenever you owe someone an answer, you hand them your preparation instead. You put being ready ahead of being on time, so the people waiting sit on your clock. Remember that you were asked for the rough version, and the rest is for you.
